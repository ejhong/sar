From orbit · what one radar image can show, and what it has not

The satellite over the same ground

Can one satellite radar image show chambers under the ground? Here the real ICEYE passes over Giza are simulated over ground whose contents are known, and the published method and a stricter reconstruction of it are run on the real images beside controls. The methods tested here do not establish subsurface imaging: their depth axis is a frequency axis of the image registration, with a repeat and a mirror, not a validated depth; the patterns their gates select also appear in images with nothing moving and nothing inside; and in our models the motion a small chamber adds at Giza's ambient level lies far below what these registrations pick up. Single images can measure strong vibration of bright targets. Where these conclusions rest on models, and what would test them, is set out below.

A satellite can measure a bright target shaking; in our tests ordinary ground’s trembling barely registers, and perfect measurements read the published way would not locate a chamber

measured · 2026 papers, P2-03

Can a satellite measure vibration from one pass?

Yes, for bright, isolated targets that move a lot: in 2026, corner reflectors on shakers and a steel footbridge were measured from orbit at 0.66 mm/s and up, to about 1 mm/s. Ordinary ground is much harder. In our test on the real Giza image, correlating one image’s looks recovered 1% of a patch of ground’s planted motion, and Giza’s natural trembling is 13,000 times below the smallest motion measured from orbit so far. The 77 µm/s once quoted here was the scatter of a reading that barely responds to the ground’s motion, not a sensitivity.

simulated · P2-06, P1-04

If every pixel’s vibration were known perfectly, would it show a chamber?

Not by the published method: its depth is the frequency of the motion, so given the exact motion at every pixel it draws the same picture over rock and over a chamber. Nor, in our simulations, by passive seismic imaging within one pass: the ground’s trembling comes in waves kilometres long, too coarse to outline a room, and a pass lasts 24.5 s. Even with short waves, perfect instruments on the ground and the rock known exactly, passive imaging shows the chamber no brighter than its surroundings after a minute of recording (1.0×), 1.9× after an hour, and never 3×.

simulated · P2-09, P2-07

Isn’t a million virtual seismometers better than a few real ones?

Only if they hear. In our test, made into 3,264 virtual sensors and shaken by a wave put into the image, they recovered 0% of it read the published way. Averaging deaf sensors gives a precise zero. Where magnitudes hear a fifth, the chamber would need 2 × 1019 sensors over it; the whole image holds 7 × 107. Over the chamber, one pass’s reading wanders 10,000 times more than a real seismometer records there.

simulated · P1-02 to P1-05, P2-03

Seismic instruments find the chamber; why doesn’t the satellite, here?

The ones that find it make their own waves. A hammer blow sends a sharp wave about 15 m long, timed exactly, and instruments pressed against the ground record its echo within 34 ms: the chamber comes back 6× brighter than its surroundings. Listening to the ground’s own trembling instead works only weakly, even with perfect instruments (2.2× with unlimited listening). A satellite has no hammer, listens for 24.5 s, registers little of ordinary ground’s trembling in our tests, and its looks follow slow motion best, a few tenths of a hertz, whose waves are kilometres long.

The real dwell has three speeds, and the simulator now has all three

A Spotlight DwellSpotlight Dwell radarAn ICEYE mode that keeps the beam on one scene for tens of seconds, here 24.5 s, far longer than a normal image needs. holds the beam on Giza for 24.5 s. Every second of aperture is also a change of viewing direction, and a look’s resolution times its duration is fixed by the orbit: a 1.96 s look resolves 0.56 m, a 0.25 s look 4.4 m. Looks are cut from the image’s Doppler band, and for each pixel every Doppler frequency is a moment of the pass, counted from that pixel’s own zero-Doppler moment: its Doppler falls by 5,662 Hz every second, so slicing the band in frequency is slicing each pixel’s 24.5 s in time, and a slice half the band wide averages 12.2 s of whatever the ground did. Across a patch the size of a pyramid those zero-Doppler moments differ by hundredths of a second.

Correlating one image’s looks registers little of ordinary ground’s motion

Every single-image method, published or ours, reads motion by registering one image’s looks against each other. Two such looks share the spectrum they overlap. For whole images and real, non-negative masks their cross-spectrum is then real and non-negative, so their correlation peaks at zero lag whatever the ground does: an identity. Registration works on small patches, which mix frequencies, so there the identity is a strong pull toward zero rather than a proof, and it has to be measured. In our model, a chamber changes the ground’s own motion by less than a ten-thousandth of it

Whatever a satellite could use to find a chamber passively would have to be its imprintimprint seismic · radarThe difference a buried chamber makes to how the surface moves as a wave passes: what any passive method would have to detect.: the difference it makes to how the surface moves. Ambient waves are far longer than the chamber and its depth, so the rock around it is strained almost uniformly and almost statically. The lab’s solver answers the static question directly: load the rock with a uniform horizontal stress, open the chamber, and let the ground settle. It is a model: one small chamber in uniform rock, strained quasi-statically; layering, the sand cover, resonance and other forcing are not in it.

The published depth is the frequency of the registration series: 6.76 m per hertz, with a repeat and a mirror

The published focusing projects each patch’s trajectory on a steering wave whose wavenumber grows with the platform’s offset, and on a dwell the offset grows steadily with time. So the focusing is a Fourier transform over time, and motion at frequency f appears at a depth of f times λs R sin θ / 2Vs, whatever is under the ground; with regular sampling each depth also has a repeat and a mirror. This is a property of the processing, not of Giza. Frequency-dependent imaging is legitimate geophysics; what is missing is a physical model showing why this relation describes a buried object, and validation on known voids. The scale depends on λs, a free parameter: this page uses the paper’s 0.48 m, and an independent review notes that the paper is inconsistent on it, its text implying 0.48 m and a displayed expression 0.24 m, the value the reconstruction uses. With 0.24 m every depth halves and nothing else changes. Given the exact motion at every pair, the focusing draws the same peak over two chambers and over open ground (Fig. S6).

The published pictures’ pillars and boxes are the method’s own arithmetic

The published Khafre renderings show tall columns, banded like spirals, with blocks at their feet. P2-08 draws the same shapes from the real Khafre image with the published focusing, and each has a plain cause. Pillars: averaged over a period of its depth axis, the power the method draws at a pixel is that pixel’s trajectory energy (correlation 1.000), so a pixel whose registration wanders is bright at every depth and a thick-slab rendering stands it up as a column. Such pixels lie where the surface is darker or changes brightness; open plateau with no monument draws them too, and uniform simulated ground draws none. Bands: along a column the power rises and falls every 13 m of the relabelled axis, its resolution. Blocks: at the surface and at each repeat depth, 648 m and 1,296 m relabelled, every steering phase is the same, so the power there is the pixel’s average offset (ratio 0.994); below 648 m the picture repeats.

Rendered as the published pictures are: a thick slab, a log scale and a rainbow palette. The claimed chambers near 1,220 m sit close to the second repeat, 1,296 m relabelled; that is suggestive only, since the published relabelling is not known exactly. In the 3-D view the method’s Khafre volume stands beside the claim. A published slice along a line is a row of pixels, each focused from its own shifts alone, so a volume is every row. The method’s “inversion” is only a Fourier transform of each pixel’s own shifts, with no waves modelled through the ground: for the Khafre patch’s47,880 pixels it takes a twentieth of a second, where a real seismic inversion of one small block takes hours (full-waveform inversion, on the seismic page). The costly step is measuring the shifts, which grows with the number of pixels; the lab refines each correlation peak locally (Guizar-Sicairos and others, 2008) instead of upsampling whole patches, so that patch took minutes, where every pixel of the whole image would take about two months.

Stricter gates select a pattern that also appears without motion; by themselves they certify neither vibration nor depth

Later versions of the method put selection gates between the shifts and the depth focus. Pairs of images are cut from the Doppler band, each 32,330 Hz of it, which for every pixel is 6.0 s of its pass, and registered to 0.01 px. A window of 50 successive pairs is kept only if the two components of its shifts trace an ellipse at one of ten modes, fitted well, with a narrow semi-axis of at least 0.005 px and at least a tenth as wide as it is long; ten windows in a row must agree on the mode, and four or five neighbouring positions must pass together. The depth focus runs only where all of that passed. We ran such a gated pipeline, unchanged, twice. The first run (P2-13) used loops the lab drew up Khafre’s faces, from 20 to 95 m, because we misread the reconstruction’s “70.5 m” as a height above the base; its own lines follow the base, 70.5 m above sea level, and the second run (P2-14, below) uses them. Both run the real 2022 image beside two motionless twins of it (its brightness and spectrum, fresh speckle), the same lines over open desert in the same image, and the real image with two stretches made to vibrate at 2 mm/s along the line of sight, some 40,000 times Giza’s microseisms, at 0.26 Hz and 3.66 Hz, resonances proposed for a 648 m column of air and for the Grand Gallery.

measured in simulation · P2-15

What the pairs register of a vibration

The two images of a pair share 98.75% of their spectrum; in the real image 36% of the shifts are exactly zero and 75% within one 0.01 px step. We first quoted a boxcar average for how much of a motion each six-second image keeps; that is the response of a rectangular average, not of this registration. Measured through the reconstruction’s own masks and registration on simulated images, each image does carry a vibration, but at 0.06 to 45 times the boxcar value depending on frequency, and a pair registers 3–46% of the shift between its two images for a bright point and 0.6–2.8% for textured ground (Fig. S9). At 2 mm/s the pair shifts change by at most 0.011 px for a bright point, about one rounding step, and 0.0017 px for texture; in the real image the planted vibrations changed them by 0.006 and 0.002 px.

For a simple target moving along the line of sight, the azimuth shift follows its velocity, times 84 s, and the range shift only its displacement, so its trace is a thin line: the moving point passes 0 of the first gate’s windows at every frequency tested, and the measurable traces with clutter or texture are at most 0.06 as wide as they are long, under the gate’s 0.1. A scene with along-track motion, several scatterers or changing reflectivity could draw other shapes; showing that would take its own forward simulation. A 3.66 Hz tone is expected to win mode 3 (3.42 cycles a window), so a mode alone cannot tell whether a planted motion came back; frequency, place, phase and amplitude together can.

why · P2-14

Its own record: each image holds six seconds of every pixel’s pass

For any one pixel, the Doppler frequency of its echo falls steadily as the satellite passes, so each frequency in its band was recorded at one moment: the pixel’s own zero-Doppler moment plus the frequency over the Doppler rate. The pipeline’s steering makes this conversion itself, with a rate of 5,390 Hz per second at Khafre, so its 32,330 Hz images each hold 6.00 s of every pixel’s pass; it puts the four sides’ zero-Doppler moments within 0.03 s of each other. The pairs are frames of a film, each a 6-second exposure, started 19 ms apart: a shiver repeating several times a second blurs out within every frame.

Planting the vibrations adds and removes a few positions (18 start passing and 15 stop, over all five supports). In the 0.26 Hz stretch the new ones sit at modes 1, 2, 3, which no 0.26 Hz motion can make; in the 3.66 Hz stretch, one at the nearest mode. 352 of the 395 positions passing at a support of one have their best depth where their mode puts it, as a frequency fit must.

What could change the answer, and where each stands

More motion

Peterson’s high-noise model, 71 times Giza’s microseisms, leaves the plateau short by 10⁷. Traffic exceeds 77 µm/s within 25 m of a road (P2-00), but at 10–30 Hz, where a look must be under 33 ms and resolves 33 m; short by 10⁴ on ground. Khafre is 199 m from the nearest road.

Brighter targets

Magnitude tracking follows an isolated bright scatterer in full (P2-03), so the best case is a stone or a placed corner reflector above the chamber: short by 10⁷ and 10⁶ under Giza’s microseisms, 10⁴ for the reflector at the noisiest stations.

Other structures

The imprint scales as volume over depth squared (checked against the solver, P2-04); the claimed deep structure scales to 0.36 of the bench chamber. Independently of any structure, Giza’s whole microseism motion is 18,365 times below the plateau’s detection threshold.

A better estimator

Under the textbook model of ordinary ground, fully developed speckle, the Doppler bins are independent circular Gaussians, so a phase history shared by a patch leaves the image’s distribution unchanged and no estimator can recover it from one image. Real ground departs from that model where it holds bright points, which magnitudes can track (above), and through the apparent brightness a displacement gradient makes, of order 10−11 here. Other departures would need their own measurement.

Stricter selection

Gates can only discard readings, never add what the image does not hold. Run unchanged on the real image, a gated version passes motionless copies of it too, passes nothing at its own settings on its own lines, and cannot pass a vibration’s own trace at any strength (above).

The published images

The pipeline draws structured sections from any input: identical with and without the chamber (P2-07), the same over monuments and open desert (R1, R6), no signal over hundreds of surveyed shafts (R13), and the published appearance everywhere (R8).

Vibration measured from orbit, 2026

The method’s authors first applied this kind of tracking to bridges in Italy and to the Mosul Dam in 2020, without instruments on the structures at the same time. Two 2026 papers (Vattulainen et al., IEEE Access; Lotti et al., Structural Control and Health Monitoring) measure vibration from single spaceborne images against ground truth: corner reflectors on shakers in open fields, and a steel footbridge chosen because it is bright. They track isolated bright targets moving at 0.7 mm/s and up, with errors near 1 mm/s: what P2-03 says works. Their smallest motion is 13,469 times Giza’s whole microseism motion and 2 × 108 times the chamber’s imprint.

Only simulation?

The coherence identity holds on the real crop to 10−9; the blindness is measured on real texture (1 ± 1%); the geometry is read from the product and reproduces its declared numbers; and the first investigation’s real-data controls found nothing at Giza or Sacsayhuamán.

What would settle it: one frozen recipe reproduced, a motion test with ground truth, and a blind test on known voids

Reproduce one nominated result

Take one figure the method’s proponents consider successful, with its image, exact geometry, code version, settings and plotting frozen and hashed, and have an independent reviewer regenerate it. Then run that frozen recipe on motionless copies, open ground and a planted motion. So far, attractive figures and negative tests have concerned different recipes.

Show that the estimator measures motion

Run the frozen pipeline on stationary scenes, on moving targets simulated from independent physics, and preferably on shaker or corner-reflector data with instruments on them at the same time. Sweep frequency and amplitude, 0.26 and 3.66 Hz included, and score recovery before the gates as well as after, beside an estimator already validated for vibrometry, so that a faulty injection is not mistaken for a failed method. Begun below with synthetic products (P2-19).

Predict known voids blind

Choose known voids and matched surveyed controls and withhold their labels. Fix the wavelength, line placement, gates and scoring in advance; predict places, depths and sizes with tolerances; count false positives as well as hits; use a site or pass not used for calibration. Before reading stairs or spirals, a forward model built on independent wave and radar physics should tell a plain column, a spiral and nothing apart.

What would change this page: a frozen recipe that passes these tests, or measurements that contradict our models, such as motion measured on the plateau during a pass, penetration measured in Mokattam limestone, or a pulse-level simulation of the pairs that registers more than ours. A bounded validation of this kind is worth more than further compelling pictures, whichever way it comes out.

The benchmark has begun: the simulator holds up against pulses, and a vibration a tracker measures leaves the gated pipeline silent

simulated pulse by pulse · P2-18

The radar simulator, checked against pulses

A second simulator computes what the radar records pulse by pulse, from every scatterer’s exact distance at each moment, displaced by its own motion, and forms the image by back-projection on the product’s grid. It focuses a point to 4.73 cm in azimuth against 4.71 cm in theory. Its frequency slices match the images formed from only the matching stretches of pulses, 0.981 to 0.997 in complex correlation, with the mirrored slices at 0.000: a Doppler slice is a stretch of the pass, shown by a calculation that never assumes it. The fast synthesizer’s image of the same scatterers matches it to 0.91, and a steady 5 mm/s recession shifts the image 41.8 cm against 42.0 cm from theory. Through the six-second pairs, pulses and the fast injection agree where the shifts are resolvable (0.094 and 0.094 px at 20 mm/s, 0.26 Hz), and the rest sit at or below one rounding step in both.

What this corrects, in the first investigation and in this lab’s own later work, and what still stands

ours · corrected

We presented impossibility more broadly than the work supports

Earlier versions of this page said one image “cannot” find a chamber and that every route was “closed”. The work shows that the methods tested do not establish subsurface imaging, that their depth axis is a frequency axis, and that the routes we modelled carry far too little. It does not prove every conceivable route impossible.

ours · corrected

We first ran the stricter reconstruction on the wrong lines

Its “70.5 m” is Khafre’s base height above sea level; we read it as a height above the base and drew loops up the faces (P2-13). Its own base lines, as sent and corrected for the geoid, are run in P2-14, with their hashes.

ours · corrected

We quoted a boxcar as the registration’s response

sinc(fT) is the response of a rectangular average. Measured through the reconstruction’s own masks and registration (P2-15), a vibrating point’s images move by 0.06 to 45 times the boxcar value, depending on frequency; the near-nulls we claimed at two of its modes are not established.

ours · corrected

We stated the shared-spectrum identity too broadly

It is exact for whole images with real, non-negative masks. Patch registration mixes frequencies, so there it is a strong pull toward zero that has to be measured, not a proof.

ours · corrected

We gave a significance to depth following the mode

We wrote “89% against 3% by chance”. After the gates keep sinusoids, depth following the mode is what the depth fit computes, not a test, and neighbouring positions are not independent findings.

ours · corrected

We leaned on two twins and one stretch of desert

They are stress tests: they show the selected pattern arises without motion or anything inside under that generator. They do not measure how often it arises over real ground, and open desert is not surveyed ground.

R9 · corrected

The coherence curve measured the looks, not the ground

Its 0.98 s to half and 1.76 s to a tenth are 1 − Δ/W for its 1.96 s looks, reproduced by simulated desert with no decorrelation and by the real crop’s own spectrum. How stable the ground is across a dwell has not been measured.

R4 · corrected

The 77 µm/s floor is noise, not sensitivity

It is the scatter of a complex registration that recovers 1% of real ground’s motion. On bright point targets a magnitude tracker does follow motion, with a scatter of the same order.

R7 · corrected

The injected motion never came back

Its recovery gains sit near zero at every amplitude up to 1 cm, as expected of motion common to a whole crop of speckle. Its later frequencies, 1, 2 and 3 Hz, sit on the nulls of its looks.

R10 · corrected

Bright pixels were graded with the same identity

Complex coherence between one image’s looks is set by the spectrum whatever the pixels are. A lone bright scatterer’s envelope in fact follows its motion across the dwell.

R13 · stands

Hundreds of surveyed burial shafts give no signal at their depths

Over the Eastern and Western Cemeteries, shafts 5 to 30 m deep, the depth profiles match bare plateau, before and after matching surface brightness.

R2, R15, R16 · stand

The depth axis is set by the processing, and resolution by the look

The published axis folds within metres and repeats by an algebraic identity (P2-06 gives it a frequency); a look short enough to follow a wave through the ground resolves tens of metres at best.
