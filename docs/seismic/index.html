Phase 1 · the reference ceiling

Seismic tomography, simulated

Before asking whether a satellite can see a chamber, ask what the instruments built for the job can see. Geophysicists image the shallow ground with arrays of geophones and a source, hammer blows or the ambient hum, and invert the records into a three-dimensional tomogram. This chapter does that in simulation, over ground whose contents are known.

Every tomogram here is scored against a truth the method never saw

The ground is composed first, from sourced materials. The waves are computed through it by a solver that knows nothing about any inversion. The inversions see only what the sensors recorded, with noise, on a different grid. Whatever they draw is then compared with the chambers that are really there.

Waves through it

3-D isotropic elastic velocity–stress finite differences, fourth order in space, absorbing boundaries on the sides and floor, a free surface on top, air-filled chambers as traction-free cavities.

What practitioners would draw

Travel-time tomography, ambient-noise tomography and full-waveform inversion, each run as it would be run in the field, then scored voxel by voxel.

Travel times catch the chamber's shadow from boreholes, and nothing from the surface

The one-chamber bench: a 6 m air-filled cube, its top 12 m down, in bare limestone. Every record comes from the solver above, with noise 30 dB below each record's peak; arrivals are picked by waveform correlation; the picks are inverted on a 1 m grid by a code that never sees the model. Air is 90% slower than the rock; a first arrival goes around the void instead of through it, so what travel times can see is the delay of the detour.

Crosshole rays through the chamber recover it as a slow patch of -11% (air would be -90%): first arrivals bend around a void, so travel-time tomography sees its shadow, not its emptiness. From the surface the same method recovers +0.0%: no first-arrival ray reaches 12 m in uniform rock.

The arrays are the ones near-surface surveys actually deploy

Active

A grid of three-component geophones at 1 to 4 m spacing and a source walked across it (hammer or weight drop), with realistic source repeatability and sensor self-noise.

Passive

A dense nodal array recording the ambient hum of traffic, wind and visitors. It is the seismic cousin of the radar claim: natural vibration asked to reveal what lies below.

Three inversions, from the classic to the state of the art

Travel-time tomography

First arrivals picked from the records, bent rays traced through an eikonal solver, a regularised least-squares update. Robust, and blurred at a wavelength.

Ambient-noise tomography

Noise cross-correlated between sensor pairs becomes the wave that would travel between them; its dispersion is mapped and inverted for shear speed with depth.

Full-waveform inversion

The whole record fitted by adjoint-state gradients, from low frequencies up. The sharpest images near-surface geophysics makes, and the most demanding.

Detectability is a map over size, depth and array, not a yes or no

For each method and each chamber: the contrast recovered at the chamber against the spread of the background, the error in position and size, and the false chambers drawn elsewhere. Swept over chamber size, depth, array aperture, spacing and frequency, the result is a map of where seismology succeeds and where it stops, the ceiling against which the satellite is judged in Phase 2.
