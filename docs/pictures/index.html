In pictures

Can one satellite image see beneath the ground?

The claim is that a single radar image, taken from orbit, holds the faint trembling of the ground, and that the right processing turns it into a picture of what lies hundreds of metres below. To test it we built ground with a chamber in it, flew the real satellite over it in a computer, and ran the published method on the picture it took. Here is what happens in our tests, one step at a time. Some steps are measured and some rest on models; each says which, and the end says what would settle it.

2

The ground is always trembling, very slightly

Ocean waves thousands of kilometres away keep every continent trembling. Near Giza the ground moves at about 0.049 micrometres per second. At that speed it would take 34 minutes to move the thickness of a sheet of paper, and it never goes anywhere: it only rocks back and forth, a few times a minute. This is the motion the claimed method says it reads.

3

In our model, a chamber changes that trembling by one part in 15,000

The waves are kilometres long, so the rock around a room 6 metres wide is squeezed and stretched almost evenly. The room gives a little as they pass, and the ground above it moves a tiny bit differently. We computed how much with the same physics engineers use for rock and steel, and checked it two independent ways. The difference is one part in 15,000 of the trembling: 3.2 × 10−6 micrometres per second.

A bigger room makes a bigger mark, but a deeper one a smaller mark: it falls with the square of the depth. The deepest structure claimed at Khafre, 80 m across at 1,220 m, would make about a third of this room’s mark.

4

What the satellite sees: the surface, grainy, and hardly anything below it

A radar image is not a photograph. Each spot is the echo of countless small reflections interfering, which gives the grainy texture radar people call speckle. The radar’s waves go only a few centimetres into dry rock. On the left is the real ICEYE image of the plateau just south-west of the pyramids; on the right, our simulated ground, flat, with the room 15 metres beneath its centre.

5

The method cuts one picture into slices and compares them

The satellite takes 24 seconds to make its picture, flying 188 km and seeing the ground from slightly different angles as it goes. The method cuts the picture into slices by the pitch of the echoes: as the satellite passes, every spot’s echo slides steadily down in pitch, like a passing train’s whistle, so each slice is made from one stretch of the pass. It then looks for the ground shifting between them. But the slices come from the same recording. Two slices of nearby moments share most of their data, so they look almost identical (correlation 0.88): comparing something with itself says “nothing moved”. Two slices seconds apart share none, and the grainy texture looks completely different from each angle (correlation 0.06): there is nothing to line up.

6

Its depth pictures are the same with the chamber and without it

Finally the method turns its readings into pictures of depth, like the ones published for Khafre. Here are its pictures of a slice down through our ground, straight through the room, processed exactly as published. With the room, and without: the same picture. The room changes the satellite’s image by 1 × 10−10 of itself, and the depth picture by 1 × 10−8. The streaks are the method’s own texture, and they look like structure anywhere.

×10 billion

That is how much the room’s effect must be multiplied before the picture changes visibly over it. Even then the change is spread evenly down the whole picture (0.68 to 1.3 times its average at every depth): the texture is shuffled, and nothing marks the room.

With the published settings the picture’s depth scale ends at 13.4 m, above our room. The setting that fixes the scale, a declared “sound wavelength”, only relabels the same picture: another choice would label it down to 650 m without changing a pixel.

7

What it calls depth is really how fast something wobbles

Why can the depth pictures not show depth? Because of how they are made. The method’s depth scale is a frequency scale in disguise: a slow wobble comes out shallow and a fast one deep, 6.8 metres for every wobble per second, whatever lies below. Give it the exact motion of the ground, which it never has, and it draws the trembling at the same depth over our room, over a room twice as deep, and over solid rock.

8

Beside a seismometer, the satellite’s reading is mostly noise

Put a seismometer on the ground over the room, and read the same spot from the satellite as the method does. The seismometer follows the trembling. The satellite’s readings scatter 10,000 times more widely, and not with it: at the scale of its readings, the ground’s real motion is the flat line along zero.

9

In our model, the chamber’s signal is three hundred million times too faint

Everything on one scale, each step ten times the last. On the left, how fast things actually move; on the right, the smallest motion one satellite pass could detect, allowing it every advantage. The chamber’s signal and the satellite’s reach are eight steps apart on plain ground: like trying to see a change the thickness of a sheet of paper on something taller than Mount Everest.

10

Could anything change the answer?

What if the ground shook harder?

At the most restless places on Earth the ground moves 70 times more than at Giza, and the chamber’s signal would still be millions of times too faint. A lorry close by shakes the ground harder, but faster than the satellite’s slices can follow; a slice short enough to follow it blurs the ground over 30 metres.

What about a shinier target?

A lone bright point is the satellite’s best case: it can follow a bright stone’s motion, even a metal reflector placed on purpose. The chamber’s signal under such a point is still 19 million times too faint for a stone and 2 million for a reflector.

What about a bigger room, or the ones claimed?

A room’s mark grows with its volume but falls with the square of its depth. The structures claimed at Khafre are far deeper; the largest, 80 m across at 1,220 m, would leave about a third of our room’s mark. And at Giza even the ground’s whole trembling is 18,000 times below what plain ground lets the satellite see, so even a room that doubled the ground’s trembling would stay invisible.

What about a cleverer way to read the image?

For ordinary ground, as radar texture is usually modelled, the information is not in the picture: motion shared by a stretch of ground leaves the texture’s statistics unchanged, a property of speckle rather than of one method. Real ground departs from that model where it has bright points, which can be tracked (above); other departures would need tests of their own.

What about millions of virtual sensors?

Every patch of a radar image can be read as a sensor, millions of them, far more than any real array, and averaging many sensors does shrink noise. So we put a wave into the image itself and read three thousand sensors: read the published way, they heard none of it. Averaging deaf sensors gives a precise zero. Where a better reading hears a little, the chamber would need 2 × 1019 sensors over it; the whole image holds 7 × 107.

Then why do the published pictures look so convincing?

Because the method draws such pictures from any image: over our flat simulated ground, with and without a room; over open desert on the real image; over hundreds of surveyed tombs at Giza, where it found none. Its tall columns are pixels whose reading wanders, drawn bright at every depth; the blocks at their feet sit where its depth scale repeats itself. How the pillars and boxes are made.

What about stricter versions that filter out the noise?

Later versions of the method keep only readings that pass a row of tests: they must circle in a clean ellipse, ten stretches in a row must agree, and neighbouring points must pass together. We ran one, unchanged, first on loops we drew up Khafre’s faces in the real image: 5 of 2,828 points passed. In copies of the image in which nothing moves, 13 and 8 passed; over open desert in the same image, none. On the lines its makers drew round the pyramid’s base, which we had first misread, with their own settings, nothing passed at all, in any of these images; loosened to single points, the real image passed 69, the still copies 28 and 29, and open desert 53. When we made the faces shake 40,000 times harder than the ground does, the tests did not find it: one new patch passed, but at a rhythm 12 times faster than the shaking planted there. Each picture the tests compare lasts six seconds, and in our measurements the pairs register little of such shaking; a simply shaking spot draws a thin line in the readings, not the round ellipse the tests ask for. What passes also passes in copies where nothing moves, and its “depth” is the rhythm the tests picked. The gates, measured.

Hasn’t vibration been measured from space?

Yes, and we agree it can be. The method’s authors first tracked bridges and a dam this way in 2020; in 2026, two teams measured metal reflectors on shakers and a steel footbridge moving about a millimetre per second, checked against instruments on the ground at the same time. That is what radar does well, a bright point moving a lot. Ordinary ground is different: its trembling at Giza is thirteen thousand times smaller than the smallest motion ever measured from orbit, and one picture barely registers ground moving at all. The chamber’s signal is two hundred million times smaller.

Instruments on the ground find the room. Why doesn’t the satellite, here?

Because the ones that find it make their own waves. A hammer blow sends a short, sharp wave, about fifteen metres long, whose timing is known exactly; instruments pressed against the ground catch its echo from the room a few hundredths of a second later. Simply listening to the ground’s own trembling works only weakly, even with perfect instruments and hours of listening. The satellite has no hammer, listens for 25 seconds, in our tests barely registers ordinary ground’s trembling, and follows slow motion best, whose waves are kilometres long.

What if the satellite could measure every point’s trembling perfectly?

The published method still wouldn’t show the room. The published method’s “depth” is only how fast things wobble, so with perfect measurements it draws the same picture over solid rock and over a room. And the ground’s natural trembling comes in waves kilometres long, far too coarse to outline a room a few metres across, during a pass that lasts 25 seconds. Even in our simulation with perfect instruments on the ground, waves short enough to outline the room, and the rock known exactly, a minute of listening doesn’t make the room stand out, and an hour only doubles its brightness.

Could the radar see down some other way?

A radar echo depends on only two things: how bright each spot is, and where it is while it is watched. This radar’s wave gets about 30 centimetres into the driest sand and less into rock; between passes, the year’s heat and the tides move the ground over a room by a few millionths of a metre, hundreds of times less than the ground’s own seasonal swelling. Only long-wavelength radar sees underground, a few metres into very dry sand. Every route, measured.

Isn’t this only a simulation?

Each step is checked on the real satellite image. The real image’s own numbers reproduce the method’s “coherence” exactly; a patch of the real image made to sway is nearly invisible to the method’s reading (1%); and the first investigation ran the method on the real Giza and Sacsayhuamán images and found nothing anywhere. The chamber’s mark and how far off it is are models, not measurements, and those two steps are only as good as the models are. What would settle it.

What does work: instruments that touch the ground

The same room, in the same simulated ground, is found by sound sent between boreholes, by the echo of a hammer blow imaged from the surface, and by fitting whole recorded waveforms. What the satellite lacks is not cleverness but contact: it sees the surface, and the surface barely feels the room.
