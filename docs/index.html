A simulation lab for the underworld

What can see beneath the ground, and how far down

In 2025 a team announced an underground city beneath Khafre's pyramid, mapped from a single satellite radar image. Katabasis tests that idea the way physics can: build ground whose contents are known, send the instruments over it, and measure what each one recovers — the seismic arrays geophysicists trust first, then the satellite.

The claim

One radar image, reprocessed, said to show a city 600 m underground

The idea is that a single satellite radar image holds the faint vibration of the ground, and that splitting it into sub-apertures turns that vibration into a picture of what lies hundreds of metres below. It was published in 2022, announced beneath Khafre in 2025, and retracted in 2026. No one has shown it working on ground whose contents are known. That is what this lab does. The claim in full

The ladder of instruments

The further an instrument sits from a chamber, the harder the chamber is to see

One chamber, 6 m across and 12 m down in limestone. Every way of looking for it, from a borehole beside it to a satellite 600 km away, is simulated over the same ground and scored against the truth.

Latest results

From orbit the chamber is out of reach; from the ground, some instruments find it

simulated and real · P2-03

One radar image cannot see the ground move

A block of the real Giza image made to sway at 2 mm/s: the published way of reading motion recovers 1% of it. A chamber changes the ground’s motion by one part in 15,000, and the published depth is only the motion’s frequency.

simulated · P1-02

Four boreholes recover the chamber's shadow; the surface sees nothing

Crosshole travel-time tomography: the chamber comes back -11% slow, where air is 90% slow — travel times see the detour, not the emptiness.

simulated · P1-03

The chamber's echo, below the noise on every record, images it from the surface

Reverse-time migration of the scattered wave, with the rock known exactly: the image is 6× brighter at the chamber than elsewhere, strongest on its top face.

simulated · P1-04

Ambient noise stands in for the hammer, but sees the chamber only faintly

Footsteps and traffic, correlated between geophones, bring back the wave a hammer would send (correlation 0.90). Imaged with the rock known exactly, the chamber stands at 2.2× the rest, where the hammer reached 6×, however long the noise is recorded.
