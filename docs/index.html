Can a satellite see beneath the ground?

The radar methods tested do not show what lies below

In 2025 a team announced an underground city beneath Khafre, mapped from one satellite radar image. We built ground whose contents we know and read it with geophones and with the satellite, beside controls.

What we found ↓Open the labThe evidence

What we found

Shaken by one vibrator, geophones find the chamber with 11 kN of force; the satellite would need 2 million times more

We built ground with a room in it, shook it with a vibrator, and read the same shaking with geophones and with a satellite, giving the satellite every advantage: corner reflectors laid over the room and a detector that already knows both answers. The whole comparison is simulated on the real satellite’s geometry.

The published method, as it stands

Its pictures come from the image and the processing, not from what lies below

  • Over a known room, at the ground’s real shaking, it draws the same picture with the room and without it; so does the stricter version.
  • Its patterns appear in motionless copies of an image and over open ground.
  • Its depth scale is set by the processing (a frequency of the registration), so absolute depth is uncalibrated and repeats. That alone does not decide whether it finds where things are, or their shape: that is tested separately.
  • What it draws across Khafre changes from one satellite pass to the next.
  • Where the data demonstrably hold a chamber, shaken hard enough for reflectors to find it, the stricter version still does not read it.

Can an underworld viewer be built?

Yes, with the right instrument for the depth

  • Instruments on the ground find the room: travel times, echoes, whole waveforms, and the vibrator above.
  • Long-wavelength radar reaches about 2 m into the driest sand at L band and 5 m at P band (X band: 30 cm); shuttle radar found buried valleys under the Sahara that way.
  • Many satellite passes measure ground that moves, as over voids that sink or collapse; a stable room in rock moves the ground 182 times less than the ground’s own seasons do.
  • One X-band image of ordinary ground: in our model, however it is read, its rate of finding the room can exceed its rate of false alarms by at most one in 770 million (step 10).

The claim

One radar image, reprocessed, said to show a city 600 m underground

The idea is that a single satellite radar image holds the faint vibration of the ground, and that splitting it into sub-apertures turns that vibration into a picture of what lies hundreds of metres below. It was published in 2022, announced beneath Khafre in 2025, and retracted in 2026. Nobody had shown it working on ground whose contents are known.

Biondi and Malanga, 2022 (arXiv:2208.00811), retracted by Remote Sensing on 10 August 2026 (notice). 1

We built the ground, so we know exactly what is in it

Solid limestone, like the Giza plateau, with one air-filled room in it: 6 metres on each side, its centre 15 metres down. Because we made it, any method’s answer can be checked against the truth.

Open it in the lab, where it turns and every instrument’s picture sits beside the truth →

2

The ground is always trembling, very slightly

Ocean waves thousands of kilometres away keep every continent trembling. Near Giza the ground moves at about 0.049 micrometres per second. At that speed it would take 34 minutes to move the thickness of a sheet of paper, and it never goes anywhere: it only rocks back and forth, a few times a minute. This is the motion the claimed method says it reads.

3

In our model, a chamber changes that trembling by one part in 15,000

The waves are kilometres long, so the rock around a room 6 metres wide is squeezed and stretched almost evenly. The room gives a little as they pass, and the ground above it moves a tiny bit differently. We computed how much with the same physics engineers use for rock and steel, and checked it two independent ways. The difference is one part in 15,000 of the trembling: 3.2 × 10−6 micrometres per second.

A bigger room makes a bigger mark, but a deeper one a smaller mark: it falls with the square of the depth. The deepest structure claimed at Khafre, 80 m across at 1,220 m, would make about a third of this room’s mark.

4

What the satellite sees: the surface, grainy, and hardly anything below it

A radar image is not a photograph. Each spot is the echo of countless small reflections interfering, which gives the grainy texture radar people call speckle. The radar’s waves go only a few centimetres into dry rock. On the left is the real ICEYE image of the plateau just south-west of the pyramids; on the right, our simulated ground, flat, with the room 15 metres beneath its centre.

5

The method cuts one picture into slices and compares them

The satellite takes 24 seconds to make its picture, flying 188 km and seeing the ground from slightly different angles as it goes. The method cuts the picture into slices by the pitch of the echoes: as the satellite passes, every spot’s echo slides steadily down in pitch, like a passing train’s whistle, so each slice is made from one stretch of the pass. It then looks for the ground shifting between them. But the slices come from the same recording. Two slices of nearby moments share most of their data, so they look almost identical (correlation 0.88): comparing something with itself says “nothing moved”. Two slices seconds apart share none, and the grainy texture looks completely different from each angle (correlation 0.06): there is nothing to line up.

6

Its depth pictures are the same with the chamber and without it

Finally the method turns its readings into pictures of depth, like the ones published for Khafre. Here are its pictures of a slice down through our ground, straight through the room, processed exactly as published. With the room, and without: the same picture. The room changes the satellite’s image by 1 × 10−10 of itself, and the depth picture by 1 × 10−8. The streaks are the method’s own texture, and they look like structure anywhere. Multiply the room’s effect 10 billion times and the picture does change, but evenly at every depth (0.68 to 1.3 times its average): nothing marks the room.

7

What it calls depth is set by how fast the ground moves: an uncalibrated scale, not by itself a useless one

The method’s depth scale is a frequency scale in disguise: a slow wobble comes out shallow and a fast one deep, 6.8 metres for every wobble per second, whatever lies below, repeating further down. Give it the exact motion of the ground, which it never has, and it draws the trembling at the same depth over our room, over a room twice as deep and over solid rock, but brighter where the ground moves more. So absolute depth is ambiguous and uncalibrated; changing the method’s sound wavelength stretches the whole scale without changing the fit. That does not show that where something is, or its outline, is lost: a method that found the right layout at the wrong depth would still be a major success. So that was tested on its own: a room, an L-shaped and a branching tunnel, their marks on the ground amplified until a detector told the shaking named every one and placed it within 0.4 m. The method’s picture, read blind, named 5 of 9, where guessing names 3 on average, and did not place them better than empty ground (the shape test, and five claims scored apart).

8

Beside a seismometer, the satellite’s reading is mostly noise

Put a seismometer on the ground over the room, and read the same spot from the satellite as the method does. The seismometer follows the trembling. The satellite’s readings scatter 10,000 times more widely, and not with it: at the scale of its readings, the ground’s real motion is the flat line along zero.

9

In our model, the chamber’s signal is three hundred million times too faint

Everything on one scale, each step ten times the last. On the left, how fast things actually move; on the right, the smallest motion one satellite pass could detect, allowing it every advantage. The chamber’s signal and the satellite’s reach are eight steps apart on plain ground: like trying to see a change the thickness of a sheet of paper on something taller than Mount Everest.

10

In our model, however the image is read, its rate of finding the room can exceed its rate of false alarms by at most one in 770 million

Steps 5 to 9 tested one method. This step bounds every method at once, including any not yet invented, within a stated model. An image is made of three things: the echo from what the radar reaches, a few centimetres into the ground; the surface itself; and how the surface moves during the 25-second pass. The room is 15 metres down, far past the echo, so only the motion can carry it; and processing can lose what the image holds, never add to it.

How much does the motion leave? Radar texture is random, like the grain of film. When the whole scene moves together, the grain stays exactly as random as it was, and the image keeps no trace of that motion: not a little, none. What leaves a trace is ground moving differently from place to place, which stretches the texture along the satellite’s track. Over the room that stretch is at most 6 × 10−11, and in our model it caps what any method can do: raise its rate of finding the room above its rate of false alarms by at most one in 770 million (with room and no room equally likely, be right half that much more often than a coin toss). At the most restless places on Earth, one in 11 million.

The cap is a bound, not what a detector achieves: on small test images the best possible detector comes within 1.4 times of it. Faster shaking reaches the room better, and the room scatters it more; a room under a roof only 5 metres thick even moves the ground over it twice as much as the passing wave. Struck directly and listened to for four seconds, the rooms we modelled rang only briefly (the longest-lived tone we could resolve had a quality factor of 2). Yet a city’s traffic hum for the whole pass leaves the cap at 2 × 10−3. Only a lorry bouncing over a bump 15 metres from the room for all 25 seconds lifts it to 0.02 (0.1 under the thin roof).

Turned around, the bound says what a detection would need. For this pass and ordinary ground, whatever pattern the room’s added motion takes, it must carry enough energy over the ground. A smooth mound of motion would need at least 47 micrometres at the lorry’s frequencies or 3 millimetres at the microseisms’; a more intricate pattern can do with less height, but not with less energy (an independent review caught our first wording, which treated the smooth pattern as the only one). Under the ground’s own trembling the room’s modelled mark falls short of that energy 460 thousand times over. Beside a bouncing lorry, with the wave the room scatters carried undiminished across the whole scene, it falls short by only 2 to 4 times, and under a 5-metre roof not at all. That is a requirement under stated conditions, not a proof about every conceivable mechanism, and it says nothing of other routes.

Hand a detector more than any method has, the exact texture of the ground, the shaking and both physical models, with the room and without, and its best is bounded exactly, whatever the texture: under Giza’s own trembling at most 1.6 × 10−6. What this leaves unresolved is strong shaking close by, known exactly: a lorry bouncing beside a room under a 5-metre roof for the whole pass lets such an oracle’s upper bound reach 0.99, so this argument does not rule out a reliable detection there (which is not the same as showing one is possible), if the wave the room scatters carries undiminished across the whole scene (within 32 metres of the room alone, far less). That is where experiments belong, not a verdict. Where a room is and its shape are bounded the same way, since telling two layouts apart is harder than telling either from none: an L-shaped tunnel from a straight one, or a column with a spiral from a plain column, and no depth allowance helps. Depth, in this model, is carried by the mark’s shape: rooms spread 15 metres down cannot mimic a room 30 metres down (71% of its mark is left over), so a reader with enough signal to detect could tell those depths apart. A method that found a layout at the wrong depth would still be useful, so the claims are scored apart.

Could anything change the answer?

What if the ground shook harder?

At the most restless places on Earth the ground moves 70 times more than at Giza’s regional level, and in our model no way of reading the image raises its rate of finding the room above its false alarms by more than one in 11 million (step 10). A lorry close by shakes the ground far harder, and faster, which the room scatters more; yet a lorry bouncing over a bump 15 metres away for the whole pass leaves that margin at most 0.02. A detection would need the room itself to add at least 47 micrometres of motion to the ground over it as a smooth mound, or the same energy in some other pattern. The method’s own slices fare worse: one short enough to follow such shaking blurs the ground over 30 metres.

What about a shinier target?

A lone bright point is the satellite’s best case: it can follow a bright stone’s motion, even a metal reflector placed on purpose. The chamber’s signal under such a point is still 19 million times too faint for a stone and 2 million for a reflector.

What about a cleverer way to read the image?

That is step 10: it bounds every way of reading one image at once, within a model. For ordinary ground, as radar texture is modelled, motion shared by the whole scene leaves the texture’s statistics unchanged, and what survives of the room’s mark lets no method, however clever, raise its rate of finding the room above its false alarms by more than one in 770 million; on small test images the best possible detector comes within 1.4 times of that bound. Bright points can be tracked, and a corner reflector over the room raises the cap only to 2 × 10−7. What the model leaves out (the echo at longer wavelengths, reflectivity changing during the pass, many images) is listed with the bound. The bound, derived and checked.

What if the room works nonlinearly, or shifts the phase of the ground’s vibration without a new tone?

Our solver is linear, so it cannot rule out an unspecified nonlinear or parametric mechanism. But any mechanism, linear or not, reaches the image only through the motion it adds to the ground over the room, and step 10’s requirement counts that motion whatever made it, by its energy, whatever its pattern. A change of phase alone, of any size, moves the ground by at most twice the vibration it acts on, so such a mechanism is ruled out only while the ground whose phase it changes stays smaller than about 55 thousand square metres at Giza’s regional levels; the room’s own footprint is about 128. A mechanism would have to show its phase changes reach that far, or have gain, drawing energy together over the room for the whole pass, and it would have to be specified well enough to compute before it could be tested.

Then why do the published pictures look so convincing?

Because the method draws such pictures from any image: over our flat simulated ground, with and without a room; over open desert on the real image; over hundreds of surveyed tombs at Giza, where it found none. Its tall columns are pixels whose reading wanders, drawn bright at every depth; the blocks at their feet sit where its depth scale repeats itself. See them under every pyramid and open plateau alike, or how the pillars and boxes are made.

What about stricter versions that filter out the noise?

Later versions keep only readings that pass a row of tests. We ran the public one, Replication and Verification of the Biondi Protocol (v1.8 standalone package), unchanged. Motionless copies of the image pass its tests too; across the pyramids it passes about one position in 8, as over open plateau; over our known room its picture changes no more with the room than with random noise of the room’s size; and across Khafre what it draws, and at what depth, does not survive a change from one satellite pass to another. The gates, measured.

Hasn’t vibration been measured from space?

Yes, and we agree it can be. In 2026 two teams measured metal reflectors on shakers and a steel footbridge moving about a millimetre per second, checked against instruments on the ground. That is what radar does well: a bright point moving a lot. Ordinary ground trembles thirteen thousand times less at Giza, and one picture barely registers it.

Instruments on the ground find the room. Why doesn’t the satellite?

Because the ones that find it make their own waves. A hammer blow sends a short, sharp wave, about fifteen metres long, whose timing is known exactly; instruments pressed against the ground catch its echo from the room. The satellite has no hammer, listens for 25 seconds, and barely registers ordinary ground’s trembling.

Isn’t this only a simulation?

Each step is checked on the real satellite image. The real image’s own numbers reproduce the method’s “coherence” exactly; a patch of the real image made to sway is nearly invisible to the method’s reading (1%). The chamber’s mark and how far off it is are models, and only as good as the models are; none of it has yet been checked against ground motion measured on the plateau during a pass. What would settle it.

What does work: instruments that touch the ground

The same room, in the same simulated ground, is found by sound sent between boreholes, by the echo of a hammer blow imaged from the surface, and by fitting whole recorded waveforms. What the satellite lacks is not cleverness but contact: it sees the surface, and the surface barely feels the room.

The lab

A lab for looking underground: known ground, every instrument, every method, and why each draws what it draws

Every site is a block diagram you can turn, cut and play waves through. Look at the ground as it is, or look with an instrument: geophones on the ground (travel times, echoes, ambient noise, full waveforms, and the waves they record), or the satellite overhead, read by the paper-style pipeline and by a stricter gated reconstruction run unchanged. Each method’s picture sits one click from its controls, with a note on why it draws the pillars, bands and columns it draws. Simulated passes and synthetic products run through the same code as the real images, and every run records the command that repeats it.
