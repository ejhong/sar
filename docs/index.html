Can a satellite see beneath the ground?

One radar image cannot see what lies below

In 2025 a team announced an underground city beneath Khafre's pyramid, mapped from a single satellite radar image. We built ground whose contents we know, flew the real satellite over it in simulation, and ran the published method. It cannot see the chamber, for three reasons that do not depend on one another, and each can be shown.

The answer

With the chamber and without it, the method draws the same picture

We built ground with a room in it, 6 m wide and 15 m down, flew the real satellite over it in simulation while the ground trembled as it does at Giza, and ran the published method on the image it took. These are its pictures of a slice straight down through the room. The dashed outline is the room.

1

It compares a picture with itself

⅛ s apart: the same data · 2 s apart: unrelated

The method cuts one image into slices and looks for the ground shifting between them. But the slices come from one recording: where they overlap they are identical, and where they don’t, nothing lines up. A patch of the real image made to sway comes back at 1%.

2

The chamber’s signal is far too faint

10⁸ times too faint on plain ground, 10⁷ on a bright stone

A room changes the ground’s own trembling by one part in 15,000. Even the whole trembling at Giza is far below what one satellite pass can detect.

3

Its “depth” is really a frequency

6.8 m of “depth” per wobble a second

Its depth scale is a frequency scale in disguise: slow wobbles come out shallow, fast ones deep, whatever lies below. Given perfect data it would still draw the same depths over rock and over a room.

The claim

One radar image, reprocessed, said to show a city 600 m underground

The idea is that a single satellite radar image holds the faint vibration of the ground, and that splitting it into sub-apertures turns that vibration into a picture of what lies hundreds of metres below. It was published in 2022, announced beneath Khafre in 2025, and retracted in 2026. Nobody had shown it working on ground whose contents are known. The claim in full

The ladder of instruments

Instruments that touch the ground find the chamber; the satellite cannot

One chamber, 6 m across and 12 m down in limestone. Every way of looking for it, from a borehole beside it to a satellite 600 km away, is simulated over the same ground and scored against the truth. The lab’s tools find it when the physics allows, so the satellite’s failure is the satellite’s.

One radar image misses the chamber three independent ways

The estimator cannot see ground move, the chamber barely changes how it moves, and the depth axis is a frequency axis. Each is sufficient alone.
